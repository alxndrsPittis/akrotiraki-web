Sifnos

Culture

Clay, music and the summer feasts: the island’s living traditions, and where to meet them.

Pottery

Sifnos has been an island of potters for a very long time. Its clay, formed from eroded shale, stands up to fire, and the island’s cooking was made for its pots: the tsikali for everyday stews, the skepastaria for the Sunday revithada, the mastelo for slow-cooked meat, the flaros chimney pot, and jugs, jars and braziers.

In the centuries of piracy the workshops stood inland, at Artemonas and Ano Petali. When the seas were safe again, the potters moved down to the shore, where boats could load their wares, and lived at the workshop through the week. From the 18th and 19th centuries many left to work elsewhere, in Attica and on other islands, so that in Greek a Sifnios came to mean simply a potter. Sifnian pottery is on Greece’s National Inventory of Intangible Cultural Heritage, and fifteen to twenty workshops still work.

The Ceramics Museum at Ktima Dragatsi in Artemonas shows the work of the island’s potters. Workshops you can visit:

Music and dance

The music of Sifnos is played by the takimi, the local pairing of violin and laouto: the violin carries the tune, the laouto the rhythm beneath it. At a feast they play through the night while the circle of dancers grows. The dances are those of the Cyclades, the syrtos and the balos among them, and the songs are mostly about love. A poem by Aristomenis Provelengios, the island’s poet, «Αέρι», was set to music and is still sung.

Older voices survive on record: Songs of Sifnos, unpublished recordings 1930–1998 gathers 21 songs from the Melpo Merlier Music Folklore Archive and other collections.

Where to hear it:

  • At the panigyria, below, through the summer.
  • MuSifanto, a classical music festival in late June and early July, held in monastery courtyards, village squares and on old paths; its closing nights have included island violin and laouto. filoitounisiou.gr/musifanto.
  • The Nikolaos Tselementes gastronomy festival in September, in the square at Artemonas, with violin and laouto and dancing every evening.
  • The Cultural Association’s dance groups, who perform traditional dances on Whit Monday.

Panigyria

Through the summer the island’s chapels keep their saints’ days. A panigyri is that feast, religious and social at once: vespers in the church on the eve, then a meal served to everyone present, at long tables in the courtyard, and music until morning. The custom is probably very old, and on Sifnos it is still very much alive.

What sets Sifnos apart is who pays. Each feast is hosted by a panigyras, a local person, a family or a group of friends and donors, who keeps the saint’s icon at home for a year and then hands it on; some churches have waiting lists of years. Not the municipality, not a sponsor: people who have taken the feast on themselves. The meal follows custom, revithada first, then meat in red sauce with pasta, or cod in fasting periods, and bread for everyone, islanders and visitors alike.

Come as a guest at someone’s celebration: with respect, modestly dressed if you go into the church, and with a plan for the ride home, since many chapels are remote and the night ends late. The feasts follow the church calendar, from Ascension, when the whole island gathers at Chrysopigi, to mid-September; dates are announced locally each year, and a calendar is kept here.

Museums and monasteries

Folklore Museum, Apollonia
On the main square: Sifnian lace, costumes and straw hats, tools, cooking pots, and an early edition of Tselementes’ cookbook. It closed for a time in 2024, so ask us whether it is open.
Chrysopigi
The 17th-century monastery of Panagia Chrysopigi, patron of the island since 1964, on a rock split in two by the sea between Platys Gialos and Faros.
Panagia Vrysiani
A 17th-century monastery at Exambela, with a museum of ecclesiastical art.
Profitis Ilias o Psilos
A fortified monastery of the mid-17th century on the island’s highest peak, reached on foot from Fyrogia in an hour or so.
